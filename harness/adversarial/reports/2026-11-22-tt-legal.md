## Findings

**1. [MAJOR] The fixed-key re-argument (ADR-020 charge 1) — ADR-020 is coherent as a risk acceptance; the risk itself is real and non-trivial.**

*Against shipping (the honest strong case):* 17 U.S.C. §1201(a)(2) forbids offering to the public, providing, or otherwise trafficking in technology "primarily designed" to circumvent (or with limited other commercially significant purpose, or marketed for circumvention) a technological measure that effectively controls access to a work; §1201(b) parallels it on the copy-control side. The T4 bridge's embedded per-year key table is, textually, a circumvention means for the AES access-control layer on `.tax` entries (which include, at minimum, Intuit-authored `manifest.xml` expression). A public GPL repository is a maximal "offer to the public" — *Universal v. Corley* (2d Cir. 2001) treated website posting as trafficking. *321 Studios v. MGM* (N.D. Cal. 2004; 9th Cir. 2004) is the controlling analog: the shipped decryptor was enjoined under (a)(2) even though end users used their own discs and had arguable fair-use purposes — user-held authorization did not save the distributor, and the court rejected importing fair use into the trafficking provisions. Public availability of the keys since 2024 does not cleanse distribution (DeCSS was also public; the ban still enjoined further distribution). This exposure is civil-injunction-grade, plus practical C&D/takedown/hosting pressure.

*For shipping:* §1201(f) has genuine texture: (f)(1) protects circumvention for identifying and analyzing elements necessary to achieve interoperability of an independently created program; (f)(2) protects developing and employing the means; and (f)(3) — the means-sharing provision — permits the information and means acquired under (f)(1)/(f)(2) to be made available to others **solely to enable interoperability** and not otherwise unlawful. A bridge whose only function is interoperation with the user's own `.tax` returns, gated by the user's own password, with refusal tests proving no bypass/recovery/brute-force path, is the strongest possible candidate for (f)(3)'s "solely" prong. The TPM-nexus cases — *Lexmark v. Static Controls* (6th Cir. 2004) and *Chamberlain v. Skylink* (Fed. Cir. 2004) — require a showing that the measure controls access to a protected work and that circumvention enables infringement; the `.tax` payload is predominantly the user's own return data, and the fixed-key layer reads more like product lock-in than copyright protection. And the copyright claim on the implementation itself fails at filtration: the format facts (container shape, record layout, key-derivation method) are §102(b) methods/ideas under *Altai*, which is why the clean-room specs-first ordering (Phase 5) matters. The keys' public documentation since 2024 (protocol §1 links the write-up, deliberately un-reproduced) also defeats any trade-secret theory and weakens "effectively controls access."

*Assessment:* ADR-020 does not claim any of this clears the bridge — it records both sides, accepts the residual risk explicitly as the owner's disposition, and binds the acceptance to conditions that narrow exposure: password-gated surface, one quarantined key module, by-existence everywhere else, refusal tests, §7.2/§7.4 unchanged, and the enforcement-response plan (C&D → pause → re-open at a gate, never silent continuation). That is exactly what a coherent risk acceptance looks like: it is not a clearance, and it says so ("NOT an ADV-LEGAL clearance", DECISIONS.md l.747). Coherent — subject to Findings 3–5 below keeping the written record airtight.

**2. [MAJOR] Canadian-law assessment (ADR-019's assigned agenda item).** Canada's Copyright Act TPM regime, added by the 2012 Copyright Modernization Act (Bill C-11):

- The **TPM definition** covers any technology/device/component that *controls access to a work* **or** *restricts doing with respect to a work anything the copyright owner has restricted* — Canada covers use-restriction TPMs, not just access controls.
- The **circumvention prohibition** (no person shall circumvent a TPM) is **civil-enforced only** — private circumvention was deliberately not criminalized.
- A separate **prohibition on providing circumvention services/technology to others** (offering, marketing, or otherwise providing services/technology that are primarily designed to circumvent, have only limited commercially significant purpose other than circumvention, or are marketed for circumvention) — mirroring §1201(a)(2)'s prongs. This is the provision that reaches a shipped bridge distributed to Canadians.
- A **closed list of enumerated circumvention exceptions** — notably **computer-program interoperability**, **encryption research**, **security testing**, and **privacy protection**, plus educational/library/archival, perceptual-disability, law-enforcement, broadcasting, and legal-proceedings categories.
- Critically, **fair dealing does not override the TPM bans** — the exceptions are the closed list only (a widely criticized feature of the 2012 Act).

*(i) T1/T2 private study of the two Canadian installers:* negligible exposure. The installers were acquired from public CDN postings (manifest URLs verified) — no access control was circumvented at acquisition; the installer containers are compressed, not password/DRM-protected, so passive survey and decompression involve decoding, not circumvention of a TPM; and the study purpose — interoperability of an independently created program — is squarely within the named interoperability exception (and encryption-research exception for the AES layer analysis). The intermediate copying posture has fair-dealing support (research/private study are named purposes) alongside the recorded Sega/Accolade, Sony/Connectix support. *(ii) The shipped bridge reaching Canadian users:* the fixed-key table is "technology" that circumvents an access-control TPM, so the providing-services/technology prohibition textually applies to global distribution. Differences vs the US: (a) civil-only enforcement — no criminal dimension for distribution; (b) Parliament affirmatively enumerated interoperability and encryption research, which supports a purposive (large-and-liberal, *CCH*-style) reading of the exception — but the exceptions protect the *person circumventing*, and unlike §1201(f)(3)'s explicit means-sharing text, the Canadian interoperability exception does not clearly license third-party *provision* of tools; (c) the closed-exception structure (no fair-dealing override) is actually worse than the US's user-side doctrine; (d) statutory damages for non-commercial infringement were capped (generally, $5,000 total) by the 2012 Act's user-protective design, though remedy calibration for TPM claims specifically is a specialist question I record as unexamined rather than assert. **Conclusion: marginal exposure vs the US analysis is comparable in principle (the distribution-side prohibition is textually at least as broad, and the means-sharing gap is the key doctrinal deficit), somewhat lower in practice** (civil-only, capped remedies, thin enforcement history, purposive sympathy for interoperability). Conditions follow: no user-facing claim of Canadian legality; Canadian notices run the same pause-and-reopen plan (no territorial distribution filtering — see Finding 5).
